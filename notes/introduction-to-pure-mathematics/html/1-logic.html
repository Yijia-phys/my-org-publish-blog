Logical Symbols

A statement is a sentence that is either true or false. We do not need to know which of these is the case for the sentence to be a statement; what matters is that it has a definite truth value.

Example 1 — Statements and non-statements

The following sentences are statements:

  1. "Every even integer greater than 222 is the sum of two prime numbers." This is a statement even though its truth has not been established.
  2. "2+3=62+3=62+3=6." This is a false statement.

The following sentences are not statements:

  1. "Is 777 a prime number?" This is a question.
  2. "x+2=5x+2=5x+2=5." Without specifying xxx or quantifying over it, this sentence does not have a definite truth value.

Mathematical sentences often contain variables. We can turn such a sentence into a statement by specifying how its variables range over a set. The symbols below let us express "for every", "there exists", and "there exists exactly one" precisely.

Definition 2 — Quantifiers

Let AAA be a set, and let P(x)P(x)P(x) be a sentence that becomes a statement whenever an element of AAA is substituted for xxx.

  1. The universal quantifier ∀\forall∀ means "for every". The statement (∀x∈A) P(x) (\forall x\in A)\,P(x) (∀x∈A)P(x) is true when P(x)P(x)P(x) is true for every element xxx of AAA.
  2. The existential quantifier ∃\exists∃ means "there exists". The statement (∃x∈A) P(x) (\exists x\in A)\,P(x) (∃x∈A)P(x) is true when P(x)P(x)P(x) is true for at least one element xxx of AAA.
  3. The unique existential quantifier ∃!\exists!∃! means "there exists exactly one". The statement (∃!x∈A) P(x) (\exists!x\in A)\,P(x) (∃!x∈A)P(x) is true when there is an element a∈Aa\in Aa∈A for which P(a)P(a)P(a) is true, and every element x∈Ax\in Ax∈A for which P(x)P(x)P(x) is true equals aaa.

We also need symbols for combining statements and for denying a statement.

Definition 3 — Disjunction, conjunction, and negation

Let PPP and QQQ be statements.

  1. The disjunction P∨QP\lor QP∨Q, read "PPP or QQQ", is true when at least one of PPP and QQQ is true. It is also true when both are true.
  2. The conjunction P∧QP\land QP∧Q, read "PPP and QQQ", is true when both PPP and QQQ are true.
  3. The negation ¬P\neg P¬P, read "not PPP", is true when PPP is false, and false when PPP is true.

We will discuss how to use these symbols in arguments and proofs later. For now, the aim is to recognise their meanings and translate ordinary mathematical sentences into symbols. Parentheses indicate which parts of a sentence belong together.

Example 4 — Translating words into symbols
  1. "Every real number is positive, zero, or negative" can be written as (∀x∈R) (x>0∨x=0∨x<0). (\forall x\in\mathbb{R})\,(x>0\lor x=0\lor x<0). (∀x∈R)(x>0∨x=0∨x<0).
  2. "There exists exactly one positive real number whose square is 111" can be written as (∃!x∈R) (x>0∧x2=1). (\exists!x\in\mathbb{R})\,(x>0\land x^2=1). (∃!x∈R)(x>0∧x2=1). The unique number is x=1x=1x=1; the other solution of x2=1x^2=1x2=1, namely x=−1x=-1x=−1, is not positive.

The truth value of a statement records whether it is true or false. We write TTT for true and FFF for false. In the classical logic used here, every statement has exactly one of these two values. The truth value of a compound statement, such as P∧QP\land QP∧Q, is determined by the truth values of its parts.

Theorem 5 — Law of excluded middle

For every statement PPP, the statement P∨¬P P\lor\neg P P∨¬P is true.

Proof.

If PPP is true, then the disjunction is true because its first part is true. If PPP is false, then ¬P\neg P¬P is true, so the disjunction is again true.

□

Definition 6 — Implication

Let PPP and QQQ be statements. The implication P  ⟹  QP\implies QP⟹Q, read "if PPP, then QQQ" or "PPP implies QQQ", is false when PPP is true and QQQ is false, and true in every other case. We call PPP the hypothesis and QQQ the conclusion.

This principle is often the basis of a mathematical proof. To show that PPP implies RRR, we can find an intermediate statement QQQ and establish both P  ⟹  QP\implies QP⟹Q and Q  ⟹  RQ\implies RQ⟹R. Repeating this process lets us build a proof as a chain of implications.

Definition 8 — Biconditional

Let PPP and QQQ be statements. The biconditional P  ⟺  QP\iff QP⟺Q, read "PPP if and only if QQQ", means (P  ⟹  Q)∧(Q  ⟹  P). (P\implies Q)\land(Q\implies P). (P⟹Q)∧(Q⟹P). Thus P  ⟺  QP\iff QP⟺Q is true exactly when PPP and QQQ have the same truth value. In this case, we say that PPP and QQQ imply each other. To prove an "if and only if" statement, we establish both directions of implication.

Properties

Disjunction and conjunction

The connectives ∨\lor∨ and ∧\land∧ obey several familiar algebraic laws. In the statements below,   ⟺  \iff⟺ means that the expressions on its two sides have the same truth value for every choice of the statements involved.

Theorem 9 — Associative, commutative, and distributive laws

Let PPP, QQQ, and RRR be statements. The following equivalences hold.

  1. Associative laws: (P∨Q)∨R  ⟺  P∨(Q∨R),(P∧Q)∧R  ⟺  P∧(Q∧R).\begin{aligned} (P\lor Q)\lor R &\iff P\lor(Q\lor R),\\ (P\land Q)\land R &\iff P\land(Q\land R). \end{aligned}(P∨Q)∨R(P∧Q)∧R​⟺P∨(Q∨R),⟺P∧(Q∧R).​
  2. Commutative laws: P∨Q  ⟺  Q∨P,P∧Q  ⟺  Q∧P.\begin{aligned} P\lor Q &\iff Q\lor P,\\ P\land Q &\iff Q\land P. \end{aligned}P∨QP∧Q​⟺Q∨P,⟺Q∧P.​
  3. Distributive laws: P∨(Q∧R)  ⟺  (P∨Q)∧(P∨R),P∧(Q∨R)  ⟺  (P∧Q)∨(P∧R).\begin{aligned} P\lor(Q\land R) &\iff (P\lor Q)\land(P\lor R),\\ P\land(Q\lor R) &\iff (P\land Q)\lor(P\land R). \end{aligned}P∨(Q∧R)P∧(Q∨R)​⟺(P∨Q)∧(P∨R),⟺(P∧Q)∨(P∧R).​
Proof.

Each equivalence is readily checked using a truth table. We omit the tables.

Definition 11 — Converse, inverse, and contrapositive

Given an implication P  ⟹  QP\implies QP⟹Q, we form three related statements:

  1. Its converse is Q  ⟹  PQ\implies PQ⟹P: "if QQQ, then PPP". We interchange the hypothesis and conclusion.
  2. Its inverse is ¬P  ⟹  ¬Q\neg P\implies\neg Q¬P⟹¬Q: "if not PPP, then not QQQ". We negate both the hypothesis and conclusion.
  3. Its contrapositive is ¬Q  ⟹  ¬P\neg Q\implies\neg P¬Q⟹¬P: "if not QQQ, then not PPP". We interchange the hypothesis and conclusion and negate both.

Their truth values are as follows:

Example 12 — Divisibility by four and by two

Consider the following statements about an integer nnn:

  1. Original: If nnn is divisible by 444, then nnn is divisible by 222.
  2. Converse: If nnn is divisible by 222, then nnn is divisible by 444.
  3. Inverse: If nnn is not divisible by 444, then nnn is not divisible by 222.
  4. Contrapositive: If nnn is not divisible by 222, then nnn is not divisible by 444.

The original implication and its contrapositive hold for every integer. The converse and inverse both fail when n=2n=2n=2: this integer is divisible by 222 but not by 444. Thus the converse and inverse are not necessarily equivalent to the original implication.

Rules for negation

Theorem 14 — Double negation

For every statement PPP, ¬(¬P)  ⟺  P. \neg(\neg P)\iff P. ¬(¬P)⟺P.

Proof.

If PPP is true, then ¬P\neg P¬P is false, so ¬(¬P)\neg(\neg P)¬(¬P) is true. If PPP is false, then ¬P\neg P¬P is true, so ¬(¬P)\neg(\neg P)¬(¬P) is false. Thus PPP and ¬(¬P)\neg(\neg P)¬(¬P) have the same truth value in both cases.

□
Remark 15 — Negating quantified statements

Let AAA be a set, and let P(x)P(x)P(x) be a sentence that becomes a statement for each x∈Ax\in Ax∈A.

  1. Negating "for every". Saying that P(x)P(x)P(x) does not hold for every element means that it fails for at least one element: ¬((∀x∈A) P(x))  ⟺  (∃x∈A) ¬P(x). \neg\bigl((\forall x\in A)\,P(x)\bigr) \iff (\exists x\in A)\,\neg P(x). ¬((∀x∈A)P(x))⟺(∃x∈A)¬P(x).
  2. Negating "there exists". Saying that no element satisfies P(x)P(x)P(x) means that every element fails to satisfy it: ¬((∃x∈A) P(x))  ⟺  (∀x∈A) ¬P(x). \neg\bigl((\exists x\in A)\,P(x)\bigr) \iff (\forall x\in A)\,\neg P(x). ¬((∃x∈A)P(x))⟺(∀x∈A)¬P(x).
  3. Negating "there exists exactly one". There must be either no element satisfying P(x)P(x)P(x) or at least two distinct elements satisfying it: ¬((∃!x∈A) P(x))  ⟺  ((∀x∈A) ¬P(x))∨((∃x,y∈A) (P(x)∧P(y)∧x≠y)).\begin{aligned} \neg\bigl((\exists!x\in A)\,P(x)\bigr) \iff{}& \bigl((\forall x\in A)\,\neg P(x)\bigr)\\ &\lor\bigl((\exists x,y\in A)\,(P(x)\land P(y)\land x\ne y)\bigr). \end{aligned}¬((∃!x∈A)P(x))⟺​((∀x∈A)¬P(x))∨((∃x,y∈A)(P(x)∧P(y)∧x=y)).​ Failure of uniqueness alone is only one possibility; existence may fail as well.
Example 16 — Examples of negating quantifiers
  1. The negation of "every real number has a nonnegative square" is "there exists a real number whose square is negative": ¬((∀x∈R) (x2≥0))  ⟺  (∃x∈R) (x2<0). \neg\bigl((\forall x\in\mathbb{R})\,(x^2\geq0)\bigr) \iff (\exists x\in\mathbb{R})\,(x^2<0). ¬((∀x∈R)(x2≥0))⟺(∃x∈R)(x2<0). The original statement is true, so its negation is false.
  2. The negation of "there exists an integer whose square is 222" is "every integer has square different from 222": ¬((∃n∈Z) (n2=2))  ⟺  (∀n∈Z) (n2≠2). \neg\bigl((\exists n\in\mathbb{Z})\,(n^2=2)\bigr) \iff (\forall n\in\mathbb{Z})\,(n^2\ne2). ¬((∃n∈Z)(n2=2))⟺(∀n∈Z)(n2=2). The original statement is false, so its negation is true.
  3. The negation of "there exists exactly one real number whose square is 111" is "either no real number has square 111, or at least two distinct real numbers have square 111": ¬((∃!x∈R) (x2=1))  ⟺  ((∀x∈R) (x2≠1))∨((∃x,y∈R) (x2=1∧y2=1∧x≠y)).\begin{aligned} \neg\bigl((\exists!x\in\mathbb{R})\,(x^2=1)\bigr) \iff{}& \bigl((\forall x\in\mathbb{R})\,(x^2\ne1)\bigr)\\ &\lor\bigl((\exists x,y\in\mathbb{R})\,(x^2=1\land y^2=1\land x\ne y)\bigr). \end{aligned}¬((∃!x∈R)(x2=1))⟺​((∀x∈R)(x2=1))∨((∃x,y∈R)(x2=1∧y2=1∧x=y)).​ Here the negation is true because 111 and −1-1−1 are two distinct solutions.

Proof techniques

Proof by contradiction

To prove a statement PPP by contradiction, we assume ¬P\neg P¬P and derive a contradiction: a statement and its negation would both have to be true. This is impossible by the meaning of negation in Definition 3. Thus ¬P\neg P¬P cannot be true, and the law of excluded middle, Theorem 5, gives PPP.

Example 17 — There is no greatest integer

There is no greatest integer.

Proof.

Suppose, for a contradiction, that there is a greatest integer NNN. Then every integer is at most NNN. But N+1N+1N+1 is an integer and N+1>NN+1>NN+1>N, contradicting the choice of NNN. Therefore there is no greatest integer.

□

Proof by contraposition

By Theorem 13, P  ⟹  QP\implies QP⟹Q is equivalent to ¬Q  ⟹  ¬P\neg Q\implies\neg P¬Q⟹¬P. To prove an implication by contraposition, we assume that its conclusion is false and show that its hypothesis must also be false. This is useful when the contrapositive is easier to prove directly.

Example 18 — An integer with an odd square is odd

If nnn is an integer and n2n^2n2 is odd, then nnn is odd.

Proof.

We prove the contrapositive. If nnn is not odd, then it is even, so n=2mn=2mn=2m for some integer mmm. Hence n2=4m2=2(2m2) n^2=4m^2=2(2m^2) n2=4m2=2(2m2) is even, and therefore is not odd. Thus an integer with an odd square must be odd.

□

Mathematical induction

Induction lets us prove a statement for every integer from a fixed starting point onwards. We establish the first case and show that truth passes from each case to the next.

Axiom 19 — Mathematical induction

Let n0n_0n0​ be an integer, and let P(n)P(n)P(n) be a statement for each integer n≥n0n\geq n_0n≥n0​. Suppose that:

  1. Base case: P(n0)P(n_0)P(n0​) is true.
  2. Inductive step: for every integer k≥n0k\geq n_0k≥n0​, the implication P(k)  ⟹  P(k+1)P(k)\implies P(k+1)P(k)⟹P(k+1) is true.

Then P(n)P(n)P(n) is true for every integer n≥n0n\geq n_0n≥n0​.

In the inductive step, we fix an arbitrary k≥n0k\geq n_0k≥n0​, assume P(k)P(k)P(k), and use this induction hypothesis to establish P(k+1)P(k+1)P(k+1). The required statement is the implication P(k)  ⟹  P(k+1)P(k)\implies P(k+1)P(k)⟹P(k+1) for every permitted kkk. Merely asserting that P(k+1)P(k+1)P(k+1) is true does not prove this step. An independent proof of P(k+1)P(k+1)P(k+1) for every such kkk would suffice, but induction allows us to use P(k)P(k)P(k).

Example 20 — Sum of the first positive integers

For every integer n≥1n\geq1n≥1, 1+2+⋯+n=n(n+1)2. 1+2+\cdots+n=\frac{n(n+1)}{2}. 1+2+⋯+n=2n(n+1)​.

Proof.

Let P(n)P(n)P(n) be the displayed identity. For the base case n=1n=1n=1, both sides equal 111.

For the inductive step, fix an integer k≥1k\geq1k≥1 and assume P(k)P(k)P(k). Then 1+2+⋯+k+(k+1)=k(k+1)2+(k+1)=(k+1)(k+2)2,\begin{aligned} 1+2+\cdots+k+(k+1) &=\frac{k(k+1)}{2}+(k+1)\\ &=\frac{(k+1)(k+2)}{2}, \end{aligned}1+2+⋯+k+(k+1)​=2k(k+1)​+(k+1)=2(k+1)(k+2)​,​ which is P(k+1)P(k+1)P(k+1). Thus P(k)  ⟹  P(k+1)P(k)\implies P(k+1)P(k)⟹P(k+1) holds for every k≥1k\geq1k≥1, and Axiom 19 proves the result.

□
Example 21 — A false induction argument: all horses have the same colour

Consider the claim P(n)P(n)P(n): "every group of nnn horses consists of horses of the same colour". The following attempted proof is a classic mistake.

The base case P(1)P(1)P(1) is true. Now assume P(k)P(k)P(k) and take k+1k+1k+1 horses, labelled h1,…,hk+1h_1,\ldots,h_{k+1}h1​,…,hk+1​. The first kkk horses have the same colour by P(k)P(k)P(k), and so do the last kkk horses. If these two groups share a horse, their colours agree, so all k+1k+1k+1 horses have the same colour.

The problem is the step from k=1k=1k=1 to k+1=2k+1=2k+1=2. The two groups are {h1}\{h_1\}{h1​} and {h2}\{h_2\}{h2​}, which share no horse. The overlap argument works only for k≥2k\geq2k≥2, so it does not establish the required implication for every k≥1k\geq1k≥1. The true base case P(1)P(1)P(1) cannot bridge this gap.

An induction proof must therefore check both the base case and the full range of the inductive step. In particular, any argument that needs overlapping groups, earlier terms, or a nonzero denominator must still work at the smallest permitted value of kkk.

Sometimes the next case is easier to prove using all preceding cases, rather than just the immediately preceding one. The following form of induction permits this.

Theorem 22 — Strong induction

Let n0n_0n0​ be an integer, and let P(n)P(n)P(n) be a statement for each integer n≥n0n\geq n_0n≥n0​. Suppose that:

  1. Base case: P(n0)P(n_0)P(n0​) is true.
  2. Inductive step: for every integer k≥n0k\geq n_0k≥n0​, if P(j)P(j)P(j) is true for every integer jjj with n0≤j≤kn_0\leq j\leq kn0​≤j≤k, then P(k+1)P(k+1)P(k+1) is true.

Then P(n)P(n)P(n) is true for every integer n≥n0n\geq n_0n≥n0​.

Proof.

For each integer k≥n0k\geq n_0k≥n0​, let Q(k)Q(k)Q(k) be the statement (∀j∈Z) ((n0≤j∧j≤k)  ⟹  P(j)). (\forall j\in\mathbb{Z})\,\bigl((n_0\leq j\land j\leq k)\implies P(j)\bigr). (∀j∈Z)((n0​≤j∧j≤k)⟹P(j)). In words, Q(k)Q(k)Q(k) says that all cases from n0n_0n0​ through kkk are true.

The base case Q(n0)Q(n_0)Q(n0​) follows from P(n0)P(n_0)P(n0​). If Q(k)Q(k)Q(k) is true, the assumed inductive step gives P(k+1)P(k+1)P(k+1). Together with the cases already contained in Q(k)Q(k)Q(k), this gives Q(k+1)Q(k+1)Q(k+1). Ordinary induction, Axiom 19, therefore proves Q(k)Q(k)Q(k) for every k≥n0k\geq n_0k≥n0​. Since Q(k)Q(k)Q(k) includes P(k)P(k)P(k), the result follows.

□
