Groups

We have met several operations, such as addition of integers and composition of functions. A group records the common rules that let us combine elements and undo these operations.

Definition 1 — Group

A group is a pair (G,⋆)(G,\star)(G,⋆) consisting of a set GGG and a binary operation ⋆:G×G→G,(x,y)↦x⋆y, \star:G\times G\to G,\qquad(x,y)\mapsto x\star y, ⋆:G×G→G,(x,y)↦x⋆y, satisfying the following three properties:

  1. Associativity. For all x,y,z∈Gx,y,z\in Gx,y,z∈G, (x⋆y)⋆z=x⋆(y⋆z). (x\star y)\star z=x\star(y\star z). (x⋆y)⋆z=x⋆(y⋆z).
  2. Identity. There exists e∈Ge\in Ge∈G such that, for every x∈Gx\in Gx∈G, e⋆x=x⋆e=x. e\star x=x\star e=x. e⋆x=x⋆e=x.
  3. Inverses. For every x∈Gx\in Gx∈G, there exists y∈Gy\in Gy∈G such that x⋆y=y⋆x=e. x\star y=y\star x=e. x⋆y=y⋆x=e. We call yyy an inverse of xxx.

The element eee is called an identity element. Closure is already included in the requirement that ⋆\star⋆ take its values in GGG.

Remark 2 — The trivial group

The one-element set {e}\{e\}{e} with e⋆e=ee\star e=ee⋆e=e is a group, called the trivial group. The identity axiom ensures that every group is nonempty. We will return to the trivial group when discussing subgroups.

Definition 4 — Abelian group

A group (G,⋆)(G,\star)(G,⋆) is abelian if its operation is also commutative: (∀x,y∈G) (x⋆y=y⋆x). (\forall x,y\in G)\,(x\star y=y\star x). (∀x,y∈G)(x⋆y=y⋆x).

Proposition 6 — Uniqueness of the identity and inverses

A group has exactly one identity element, and every element has exactly one inverse.

Remark 8 — Multiplicative and additive notation

In multiplicative notation, we write the operation as xyxyxy, the identity as eee or 111, and the inverse as x−1x^{-1}x−1. The operation need not be ordinary multiplication, and the group need not be abelian. The power rules above concern repeated copies of a single element; they do not assert that arbitrary elements commute.

For abelian groups, we often use additive notation: the operation is x+yx+yx+y, the identity is 000, and the inverse is −x-x−x. Repeated addition is written nxnxnx, with 0x=00x=00x=0 and (−n)x=n(−x)(-n)x=n(-x)(−n)x=n(−x). The power laws then become (−m)x=−(mx),mx+nx=(m+n)x. (-m)x=-(mx),\qquad mx+nx=(m+n)x. (−m)x=−(mx),mx+nx=(m+n)x. These are conventions for writing the group operation, even when its elements are not numbers.

Permutations of a set

Definition 9 — Permutations and symmetric groups

A permutation of a set XXX is a bijection X→XX\to XX→X. The symmetric group on XXX is the set Sym⁡(X)={σ:X→X∣σ is bijective}, \operatorname{Sym}(X)=\{\sigma:X\to X\mid\sigma\text{ is bijective}\}, Sym(X)={σ:X→X∣σ is bijective}, with composition as its operation. Definition 10 — Adjacency-preserving permutation

Suppose a set XXX has a specified adjacency relation, written x∼yx\sim yx∼y. A permutation σ\sigmaσ preserves adjacency if (∀x,y∈X) (x∼y  ⟺  σ(x)∼σ(y)). Remark 11 — Preserving other structures

We can similarly study permutations that preserve distances, colours, an order, or an algebraic operation. For example, distance preservation means d(σ(x),σ(y))=d(x,y)d(\sigma(x),\sigma(y))=d(x,y)d(σ(x),σ(y))=d(x,y), while preservation of a binary operation means σ(x⋆y)=σ(x)⋆σ(y)\sigma(x\star y)=\sigma(x)\star\sigma(y)σ(x⋆y)=σ(x)⋆σ(y). The permutations preserving a specified structure in both directions form a group under composition, often called its automorphism group.

Cycles in the symmetric group

Definition 12 — Cycle

Let 1≤k≤n1\leq k\leq n1≤k≤n and let a1,…,aka_1,\ldots,a_ka1​,…,ak​ be distinct elements of {1,…,n}\{1,\ldots,n\}{1,…,n}. Remark 13 — Two-row notation and cycle notation

A permutation can be written in two-row notation: σ=(12⋯nσ(1)σ(2)⋯σ(n)). \sigma=\begin{pmatrix} 1&2&\cdots&n\\ \sigma(1)&\sigma(2)&\cdots&\sigma(n) \end{pmatrix}. σ=(1σ(1)​2σ(2)​⋯⋯​nσ(n)​). For example, (12343241)=(1 3 4), \begin{pmatrix}1&2&3&4\\3&2&4&1\end{pmatrix}=(1\ 3\ 4), (13​22​34​41​)=(1 3 4), with 222 fixed. Products of cycles are read from right to left, just like other compositions.

Definition 14 — Disjoint cycles

Two cycles are disjoint if no symbol occurs in both lists. A collection of cycles is pairwise disjoint if every two distinct cycles in it are disjoint.

Lemma 15 — Disjoint cycles commute

If σ,τ∈Sn\sigma,\tau\in S_nσ,τ∈Sn​ are disjoint cycles, then στ=τσ\sigma\tau=\tau\sigmaστ=τσ.

Proof.

If xxx occurs in σ\sigmaσ, then τ\tauτ fixes both xxx and σ(x)\sigma(x)σ(x), since σ(x)\sigma(x)σ(x) also occurs in σ\sigmaσ. Thus both compositions send xxx to σ(x)\sigma(x)σ(x). Definition 18 — Dihedral group

Let n≥3n\geq3n≥3, and label the vertices of a regular nnn-gon v1,…,vnv_1,\ldots,v_nv1​,…,vn​ in counterclockwise order. The dihedral group D2nD_{2n}D2n​ consists of the permutations of its vertices induced by the geometric symmetries of the polygon, with composition as its operation.

A geometric symmetry is a distance-preserving transformation of the plane taking the polygon onto itself. Such transformations preserve adjacency; their compositions and inverses are again symmetries. Our notation D2nD_{2n}D2n​ uses the number of elements, as the next result verifies.

Remark 32 — Finite order does not make the group finite

An element can have finite order in an infinite group. For example, −1-1−1 has order 222 in the infinite group (R∖{0},⋅)(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\},\cdot)(R∖{0},⋅). Definition 42 — Isomorphism and isomorphic groups

An isomorphism ϕ:G→H\phi:G\to Hϕ:G→H is a bijective homomorphism. Groups GGG and HHH are isomorphic, written G≅HG\cong HG≅H, if there exists an isomorphism between them.

Remark 43 — The terminology

The names come from Greek: homo- means "same", iso- means "equal", and morph- refers to form or shape. Thus the words suggest "same form" and "equal form". Formally, a homomorphism preserves the group operation, while an isomorphism does so bijectively, allowing the two groups to be viewed as the same group structure with different labels.
