Basic properties

Throughout this chapter, nnn is a positive integer. We regard a matrix as an ordered list of its rows v1,…,vn∈Rnv_1,\ldots,v_n\in\mathbb{R}^nv1​,…,vn​∈Rn. Write eje_jej​ for the vector with 111 in coordinate jjj and 000 elsewhere.

Definition 1 — The determinant of order n

A determinant of order n is a function d:(Rn)n→R d:(\mathbb{R}^n)^n\to\mathbb{R} d:(Rn)n→R satisfying the following three properties.

  1. Multilinearity. It is linear in each row separately. With the other rows fixed, for u,v∈Rnu,v\in\mathbb{R}^nu,v∈Rn and α,β∈R\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}α,β∈R, d(v1,…,αu+βv,…,vn)=αd(v1,…,u,…,vn)+βd(v1,…,v,…,vn). d(v_1,\ldots,\alpha u+\beta v,\ldots,v_n) =\alpha d(v_1,\ldots,u,\ldots,v_n) +\beta d(v_1,\ldots,v,\ldots,v_n). d(v1​,…,αu+βv,…,vn​)=αd(v1​,…,u,…,vn​)+βd(v1​,…,v,…,vn​).
  2. Change of sign. Interchanging any two distinct rows changes the sign: d(…,vi,…,vj,…)=−d(…,vj,…,vi,…). d(\ldots,v_i,\ldots,v_j,\ldots) =-d(\ldots,v_j,\ldots,v_i,\ldots). d(…,vi​,…,vj​,…)=−d(…,vj​,…,vi​,…).
  3. Normalization. d(e1,…,en)=1d(e_1,\ldots,e_n)=1d(e1​,…,en​)=1.

For a matrix AAA with rows v1,…,vnv_1,\ldots,v_nv1​,…,vn​, we write det⁡A\det AdetA or det⁡(v1,…,vn)\det(v_1,\ldots,v_n)det(v1​,…,vn​) for d(v1,…,vn)d(v_1,\ldots,v_n)d(v1​,…,vn​). Thus we can also view the determinant as a function Mn,n(R)→RM_{n,n}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}Mn,n​(R)→R, with det⁡In=1\det I_n=1detIn​=1.

Linearity applies to one row at a time, keeping all other rows fixed. We will prove below that these three properties determine exactly one function.

Proposition 3 — Consequences of the defining properties

For a square matrix with rows v1,…,vnv_1,\ldots,v_nv1​,…,vn​:

  1. If one row is the zero vector, its determinant is zero.
  2. If two distinct rows are equal, its determinant is zero.
  3. If one row is a scalar multiple of another distinct row, its determinant is zero.
  4. Adding any scalar multiple of one row to another distinct row leaves its determinant unchanged: d(…,vi+cvj,…,vj,…)=d(…,vi,…,vj,…)(i≠j). d(\ldots,v_i+cv_j,\ldots,v_j,\ldots) =d(\ldots,v_i,\ldots,v_j,\ldots) \qquad(i\ne j). d(…,vi​+cvj​,…,vj​,…)=d(…,vi​,…,vj​,…)(i=j).
Proof.

For the first assertion, write the zero row as 0u0u0u. Linearity in that row makes the determinant 000 times a real number, hence zero.

For the second, swapping the equal rows shows that the determinant equals its own negative, so it is zero.

For the third, if vi=cvjv_i=cv_jvi​=cvj​, pull the scalar ccc out of row iii. The remaining determinant has two equal rows and is zero.

Finally, linearity in row iii gives d(…,vi+cvj,…,vj,…)=d(…,vi,…,vj,…)+c d(…,vj,…,vj,…).\begin{aligned} d(\ldots,v_i+cv_j,\ldots,v_j,\ldots) &=d(\ldots,v_i,\ldots,v_j,\ldots)\\ &\quad+c\,d(\ldots,v_j,\ldots,v_j,\ldots). \end{aligned}d(…,vi​+cvj​,…,vj​,…)​=d(…,vi​,…,vj​,…)+cd(…,vj​,…,vj​,…).​ The last determinant is zero by the second assertion.

Remark 4 — Effects of elementary row operations

The three elementary row operations affect the determinant as follows:

  1. Interchanging two rows multiplies it by −1-1−1.
  2. Multiplying one row by c≠0c\ne0c=0 multiplies it by ccc.
  3. Adding a multiple of one row to a different row leaves it unchanged.

These rules let us keep track of the determinant during row reduction.

Theorem 5 — Existence and uniqueness of the determinant

For every positive integer nnn, there exists exactly one determinant of order nnn.

Proof.

Remark 20 — Calculation in practice

For larger matrices, we usually calculate determinants by row reduction to triangular form. Repeated Laplace expansion creates many smaller determinants, and constructing the whole adjugate requires computing many cofactors. These formulas are useful for explicit identities; elimination is generally the more efficient way to calculate a determinant or solve a system.

Proposition 25 — The cross product and the angle

Remark 26 — Why the dimension matters

In three dimensions, two nonparallel vectors determine a plane, and the vectors perpendicular to that plane lie along one line. The cross product chooses a direction on that line by the right-hand rule, with length equal to the parallelogram's area.

In two dimensions, there is no nonzero vector perpendicular to two nonparallel vectors. In higher dimensions, there are more perpendicular directions, so perpendicularity and length no longer determine a vector up to sign. The three-coordinate formula therefore does not directly extend to arbitrary dimensions. A special analogue exists in dimension 777, but we do not discuss it here.
