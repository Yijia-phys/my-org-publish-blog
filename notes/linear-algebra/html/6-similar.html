Definition 1 — Change-of-basis matrix

Let A=(v1,…,vn)A=(v_1,\ldots,v_n)A=(v1​,…,vn​) and B=(w1,…,wn)B=(w_1,\ldots,w_n)B=(w1​,…,wn​) be ordered bases of a vector space VVV over KKK. Remark 2 — The standard basis

For KnK^nKn, we denote the standard basis by E=(e1,…,en)\mathcal{E}=(e_1,\ldots,e_n)E=(e1​,…,en​). Then (x)E(x)_{\mathcal{E}}(x)E​ is the vector with its usual coordinate column xxx.

To construct CE←AC_{\mathcal{E}\leftarrow A}CE←A​, place the basis vectors of AAA into columns, using their standard coordinates. It is an nnn by nnn matrix, with one column for each vector of AAA.

Remark 3 — Bases are ordered here

Throughout this chapter, a basis comes with a specified order. The first coordinate multiplies the first basis vector, and so on. Reordering a basis changes the corresponding coordinate columns and change-of-basis matrices.

Proposition 7 — Coordinate conversion and the identity matrix
  1. Proposition 35 — Number of distinct eigenvalues

Remark 36 — Fundamental theorem of algebra

The fundamental theorem of algebra, classically associated with Gauss, states that every nonconstant polynomial with complex coefficients has a complex root. Repeated factoring therefore gives exactly as many roots as its degree, counting repetitions. In our convention, over C\mathbb CC we can write χT(t)=∏i=1n(λi−t), \chi_T(t)=\prod_{i=1}^n(\lambda_i-t), χT​(t)=i=1∏n​(λi​−t), where the λi\lambda_iλi​ may repeat.

The lower bound of one eigenvalue need not hold over R\mathbb RR. For example, X=(0−110)X=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}X=(01​−10​) has characteristic polynomial t2+1t^2+1t2+1, so it has no real eigenvalues.
