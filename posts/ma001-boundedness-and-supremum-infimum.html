1. Foundations

Let us begin with a brief review of the definitions.

Definition 1 — Boundedness

Let \(A \subseteq \mathbb{R}\). We say that \(A\) is bounded above if there exists \(M \in \mathbb{R}\) such that \(a \leq M\) for every \(a \in A\). Any such \(M\) is called an upper bound of \(A\).

Similarly, \(A\) is bounded below if there exists \(m \in \mathbb{R}\) such that \(m \leq a\) for every \(a \in A\). Any such \(m\) is called a lower bound of \(A\).

The set \(A\) is bounded if it is both bounded above and bounded below. Thus,

\[ A \text{ is bounded} \iff \bigl(A \text{ is bounded above}\bigr) \land \bigl(A \text{ is bounded below}\bigr). \]

In particular, the empty set is bounded: every real number is both an upper bound and a lower bound of \(\varnothing\), since there are no elements that could violate either condition.

Definition 2 — Supremum and infimum

Let \(A \subseteq \mathbb{R}\). A real number \(s\) is the supremum (or least upper bound) of \(A\), written \(s = \sup A\), if:

  1. \(a \leq s\) for every \(a \in A\);
  2. \(s \leq M\) for every upper bound \(M \in \mathbb{R}\) of \(A\).

Similarly, a real number \(t\) is the infimum (or greatest lower bound) of \(A\), written \(t = \inf A\), if:

  1. \(t \leq a\) for every \(a \in A\);
  2. \(m \leq t\) for every lower bound \(m \in \mathbb{R}\) of \(A\).

To include unbounded sets and the empty set, we also allow values in the extended real line \(\overline{\mathbb{R}} = \mathbb{R} \cup \{-\infty,+\infty\}\), ordered by \(-\infty \lt x \lt +\infty\) for every \(x \in \mathbb{R}\). We adopt the conventions

\[\begin{aligned} \sup A &= +\infty &&\text{if } A \text{ is not bounded above}, \\ \inf A &= -\infty &&\text{if } A \text{ is not bounded below}, \\ \sup \varnothing &= -\infty, \qquad &\inf \varnothing &= +\infty. \end{aligned}\]

These conventions agree with taking the least upper bound and greatest lower bound in \(\overline{\mathbb{R}}\). The empty set has no supremum or infimum in \(\mathbb{R}\) itself.

Axiom 3 — Completeness of the real numbers

Every nonempty subset of \(\mathbb{R}\) that is bounded above has a supremum in \(\mathbb{R}\).

Every nonempty subset of \(\mathbb{R}\) that is bounded below has an infimum in \(\mathbb{R}\).

The second statement follows from the first by applying it to \(-A = \{-a : a \in A\}\) and using \(\inf A = -\sup(-A)\). We state both together here for ease of reference.

Theorem 4 — Archimedean property

For every \(x \in \mathbb{R}\), there exists a positive integer \(n\) such that \(x \lt n\). In other words, the positive integers are not bounded above in \(\mathbb{R}\).

Lemma 5 — Integer bracketing

For every \(x \in \mathbb{R}\), there exists an integer \(n \in \mathbb{Z}\) such that

\[ n - 1 \leq x \lt n. \]

We will not go into further proofs or explanations here; for details, see the notes.

2. Proof Strategies

We now examine in detail how to prove the various properties introduced above.

2.1. Boundedness

To prove boundedness, we find fixed real bounds that work for every element of the set.

Remark 6

Let \(A\subseteq\mathbb{R}\). Then

\[\begin{aligned} A\text{ is bounded above} &\iff \exists M\in\mathbb{R}\;\forall a\in A,\quad a\leq M, \\ A\text{ is bounded below} &\iff \exists m\in\mathbb{R}\;\forall a\in A,\quad m\leq a, \\ A\text{ is bounded} &\iff \exists m,M\in\mathbb{R}\;\forall a\in A,\quad m\leq a\leq M. \end{aligned}\]

The bounds may depend on the set and its fixed parameters, but must not depend on the individual element \(a\). We choose the bounds first and then verify the inequalities for arbitrary \(a\in A\); there is no need to find the best possible bounds.

2.2. Unboundedness

First, negating the definitions of boundedness above and below gives the following characterizations.

Remark 7

Let \(A \subseteq \mathbb{R}\). Then

\[\begin{aligned} A \text{ is unbounded above} &\iff \neg\bigl(\exists M \in \mathbb{R}\; \forall a \in A,\; a \leq M\bigr) \\ &\iff \forall M \in \mathbb{R}\; \exists a \in A,\; a > M, \\ A \text{ is unbounded below} &\iff \neg\bigl(\exists m \in \mathbb{R}\; \forall a \in A,\; m \leq a\bigr) \\ &\iff \forall m \in \mathbb{R}\; \exists a \in A,\; a \lt m. \end{aligned}\]

In other words, to prove that \(A\) is unbounded above, we start with an arbitrary proposed upper bound \(M \in \mathbb{R}\) and find an element \(a \in A\) with \(a > M\). Our choice of \(a\) may depend on \(M\): when giving an explicit construction, we express \(a\) in terms of \(M\) and verify both that \(a \in A\) and that \(a > M\).

Likewise, to prove that \(A\) is unbounded below, we start with an arbitrary \(m \in \mathbb{R}\) and choose \(a \in A\), depending on \(m\), such that \(a \lt m\). The order of the quantifiers matters: the proposed bound is given first, and we then choose an element that exceeds it or falls below it.

Let us illustrate this with a simple example.

Example 8 — The real line is unbounded

The set \(A = \{x \mid x \in \mathbb{R}\} = \mathbb{R}\) is unbounded both above and below.

Proof.

Given any \(M \in \mathbb{R}\), choose \(a = M + 1\). Then \(a \in A\) and \(a > M\), so \(A\) is unbounded above.

Similarly, given any \(m \in \mathbb{R}\), choose \(a = m - 1\). Then \(a \in A\) and \(a \lt m\), so \(A\) is unbounded below. Hence \(A\) is unbounded.

□

2.3. Identifying a Supremum or Infimum

To prove that a real number \(s\) is the supremum of \(A\), we must show that it is an upper bound and that it is the least upper bound. For the second part, we show that every number strictly smaller than \(s\), written as \(s - \varepsilon\) with \(\varepsilon > 0\), fails to be an upper bound.

Remark 9

Let \(A \subseteq \mathbb{R}\) and \(s,t \in \mathbb{R}\). Then \(s = \sup A\) if and only if both of the following hold:

  1. \(s\) is an upper bound: \[ \forall a \in A,\quad a \leq s. \]
  2. For every \(\varepsilon > 0\), \(s - \varepsilon\) is not an upper bound: \[ \forall \varepsilon > 0\; \exists a \in A,\quad s - \varepsilon \lt a. \]

Similarly, \(t = \inf A\) if and only if both of the following hold:

  1. \(t\) is a lower bound: \[ \forall a \in A,\quad t \leq a. \]
  2. For every \(\varepsilon > 0\), \(t + \varepsilon\) is not a lower bound: \[ \forall \varepsilon > 0\; \exists a \in A,\quad a \lt t + \varepsilon. \]

The two parts require different approaches. To establish the bound, take an arbitrary \(a \in A\) and prove the required inequality. To establish that the bound is the least or greatest one, take an arbitrary \(\varepsilon > 0\) and find a suitable \(a \in A\). Here the candidate \(s\) or \(t\) is fixed, and our choice of \(a\) may depend on \(\varepsilon\): in an explicit construction, we express \(a\) in terms of \(\varepsilon\) (and the fixed candidate \(s\) or \(t\)).

For a supremum, the chosen element must satisfy \(s - \varepsilon \lt a \leq s\); for an infimum, it must satisfy \(t \leq a \lt t + \varepsilon\). In each case, we must verify that the chosen element belongs to \(A\). The element need not equal the supremum or infimum; it only needs to lie within the prescribed distance on the appropriate side.

Example 10 — A supremum that is not attained

Let

\[ A = \left\{1 - \frac{1}{x} \;\middle|\; x \in \mathbb{R}_{+}\right\}, \]

where \(\mathbb{R}_{+} = (0,\infty)\). Then \(\sup A = 1\).

Proof.

First, for every \(x > 0\), we have \(1/x > 0\), so \(1 - 1/x \lt 1\). Thus every \(a \in A\) satisfies \(a \leq 1\), and \(1\) is an upper bound of \(A\).

Next, let \(\varepsilon > 0\) be arbitrary. To show that \(1 - \varepsilon\) is not an upper bound, we need an \(x > 0\) such that

\[ 1 - \frac{1}{x} > 1 - \varepsilon, \]

which is equivalent to \(x > 1/\varepsilon\). Choose \(x = 2/\varepsilon > 0\). Then \(a = 1 - 1/x\) belongs to \(A\) and

\[ a = 1 - \frac{\varepsilon}{2} > 1 - \varepsilon. \]

Hence \(1 - \varepsilon\) is not an upper bound for any \(\varepsilon > 0\). Therefore \(\sup A = 1\).

□

In Section 3, we will discuss more systematically how to solve inequalities of this kind and choose suitable elements of the set.

There is also a simpler way to identify a supremum when the proposed upper bound belongs to the set itself.

Theorem 11 — Attained bounds are suprema and infima

Let \(A \subseteq \mathbb{R}\) and \(s,t \in \mathbb{R}\). If \(s\) is an upper bound of \(A\) and \(s \in A\), then \(\sup A = s\).

Similarly, if \(t\) is a lower bound of \(A\) and \(t \in A\), then \(\inf A = t\).

The converse does not hold: \(s = \sup A\) does not imply that \(s \in A\). In the preceding example, \(\sup A = 1\), but \(1 \notin A\), since \(1 - 1/x \lt 1\) for every \(x > 0\).

2.4. Ruling Out a Candidate Supremum or Infimum

Taking the contrapositives of the necessary conditions in Section 2.3 gives two ways to rule out a proposed supremum or infimum. It is enough for either condition to fail.

Remark 12

Let \(A \subseteq \mathbb{R}\) and let \(s,t \in \mathbb{R}\) be arbitrary. Then \(s\) is not the supremum of \(A\) if either of the following holds:

  1. There exists \(\varepsilon > 0\) such that \(s + \varepsilon \in A\), so \(s\) is not an upper bound.
  2. There exists \(\varepsilon > 0\) such that \(s - \varepsilon\) is an upper bound of \(A\), so \(s\) cannot be the least upper bound.

In logical notation,

\[\begin{aligned} \Bigl[\bigl(\exists \varepsilon > 0,\; s + \varepsilon \in A\bigr) \lor \bigl(\exists \varepsilon > 0\; \forall a \in A,\; a \leq s - \varepsilon\bigr)\Bigr] \\ \iff s \neq \sup A. \end{aligned}\]

Similarly, \(t\) is not the infimum of \(A\) if either of the following holds:

  1. There exists \(\varepsilon > 0\) such that \(t - \varepsilon \in A\), so \(t\) is not a lower bound.
  2. There exists \(\varepsilon > 0\) such that \(t + \varepsilon\) is a lower bound of \(A\), so \(t\) cannot be the greatest lower bound.

In logical notation,

\[\begin{aligned} \Bigl[\bigl(\exists \varepsilon > 0,\; t - \varepsilon \in A\bigr) \lor \bigl(\exists \varepsilon > 0\; \forall a \in A,\; t + \varepsilon \leq a\bigr)\Bigr] \\ \iff t \neq \inf A. \end{aligned}\]

The dependence has now changed. In Section 2.3, we were given an arbitrary \(\varepsilon > 0\) and had to choose an element \(a\) in terms of it. Here the candidate \(s\) or \(t\) is fixed, and we only need to find one suitable \(\varepsilon > 0\), chosen in terms of that candidate, to rule it out. In the second case, this single choice must work for every \(a \in A\), since we must show that the shifted candidate is still a bound.

Example 13 — Constructing a cube root using completeness

Let

\[ A = \{x \in \mathbb{R} \mid x^3 \lt 2\}. \]

Then \(s = \sup A\) exists and satisfies \(s^3 = 2\).

Proof.

Since \(1 \in A\) and \(2\) is an upper bound of \(A\), completeness gives \(s = \sup A \in \mathbb{R}\). Moreover, \(1 \leq s \leq 2\), so \(s > 0\).

Suppose first that \(s^3 > 2\). Choose

\[ \varepsilon_1 = \min\left\{\frac{s}{2},\frac{s^3 - 2}{6s^2}\right\}. \]

Then \(0 \lt \varepsilon_1 \leq s/2 \lt s\), and

\[\begin{aligned} (s - \varepsilon_1)^3 &= s^3 - 3s^2\varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_1^2(3s - \varepsilon_1) \\ &\geq s^3 - 3s^2\varepsilon_1 \\ &\geq s^3 - \frac{s^3 - 2}{2} \\ &= \frac{s^3 + 2}{2} > 2. \end{aligned}\]

For every \(a \in A\), we therefore have \(a^3 \lt 2 \lt (s - \varepsilon_1)^3\). Since cubing is strictly increasing on \(\mathbb{R}\), it follows that \(a \lt s - \varepsilon_1\). Thus \(s - \varepsilon_1\) is an upper bound of \(A\), contradicting the fact that \(s\) is its least upper bound.

Suppose instead that \(s^3 \lt 2\). Choose

\[ \varepsilon_2 = \min\left\{\frac{s}{2},\frac{2 - s^3}{14s^2}\right\}. \]

Again, \(0 \lt \varepsilon_2 \leq s/2 \lt s\). Using \(\varepsilon_2 \lt s\), we obtain

\[\begin{aligned} (s + \varepsilon_2)^3 &= s^3 + 3s^2\varepsilon_2 + 3s\varepsilon_2^2 + \varepsilon_2^3 \\ &\leq s^3 + 7s^2\varepsilon_2 \\ &\leq s^3 + \frac{2 - s^3}{2} \\ &= \frac{s^3 + 2}{2} \lt 2. \end{aligned}\]

Hence \(s + \varepsilon_2 \in A\). But \(s + \varepsilon_2 > s\), contradicting the fact that \(s\) is an upper bound of \(A\).

Both alternatives are impossible, so \(s^3 = 2\).

□

For now, we have simply stated suitable choices of \(\varepsilon_1\) and \(\varepsilon_2\) and checked that they work. In Section 3, we will explain how to obtain these choices systematically.

2.5. Using Known Suprema and Infima

Corollary 14 — Approximating suprema and infima by elements of the set

Let \(A \subseteq \mathbb{R}\) and \(s,t \in \mathbb{R}\).

If \(s = \sup A\), then for every \(\varepsilon > 0\) there exists \(x \in A\) such that

\[ s - \varepsilon \lt x \leq s. \]

Similarly, if \(t = \inf A\), then for every \(\varepsilon > 0\) there exists \(x \in A\) such that

\[ t \leq x \lt t + \varepsilon. \]

In logical notation,

\[\begin{aligned} s = \sup A &\implies \forall \varepsilon > 0\; \exists x \in A,\quad s - \varepsilon \lt x \leq s, \\ t = \inf A &\implies \forall \varepsilon > 0\; \exists x \in A,\quad t \leq x \lt t + \varepsilon. \end{aligned}\]

Once we know that \(s = \sup A\) or \(t = \inf A\), we can use the corresponding approximation property as an available hypothesis in another proof. We choose a positive tolerance suited to the problem, and the corollary supplies an element of \(A\) within that tolerance of the known supremum or infimum.

Example 15 — The supremum of a reciprocal set

Let \(\varnothing \neq A \subseteq \mathbb{R}_{+}\) and define

\[ B = \left\{\frac{1}{x} \;\middle|\; x \in A\right\}. \]

If \(t = \inf A\) and \(t \neq 0\), then \(\sup B = 1/t\).

Proof.

Since \(A \subseteq (0,\infty)\), we have \(t \geq 0\). Together with \(t \neq 0\), this gives \(t > 0\).

First, every \(a \in A\) satisfies \(a \geq t > 0\), so \(1/a \leq 1/t\). Thus \(1/t\) is an upper bound of \(B\).

Next, let \(\varepsilon > 0\) be arbitrary. Since \(t^2\varepsilon > 0\) and \(t = \inf A\), we can apply the corollary with tolerance \(t^2\varepsilon\) to obtain an \(a \in A\) such that

\[ t \leq a \lt t + t^2\varepsilon. \]

Using \(a \geq t > 0\) and \(a - t \lt t^2\varepsilon\), we obtain

\[ 0 \leq \frac{1}{t} - \frac{1}{a} = \frac{a-t}{ta} \leq \frac{a-t}{t^2} \lt \frac{t^2\varepsilon}{t^2} = \varepsilon. \]

Therefore \(1/t - \varepsilon \lt 1/a \leq 1/t\), with \(1/a \in B\). Hence \(1/t - \varepsilon\) is not an upper bound of \(B\). Since this holds for every \(\varepsilon > 0\), we conclude that \(\sup B = 1/t\).

□

2.6. Summary

The following table summarizes the logical goals and the order in which we choose the quantities involved. In the last two rows, the supremum or infimum is already known, so the displayed statement is available for use in a proof.

Name Logical goal What depends on what?
Bounded above \(\exists M\in\mathbb{R}\;\forall a\in A,\;a\leq M\) Choose a fixed \(M\) independent of \(a\), then verify the inequality for every \(a\in A\).
Bounded below \(\exists m\in\mathbb{R}\;\forall a\in A,\;m\leq a\) Choose a fixed \(m\) independent of \(a\), then verify the inequality for every \(a\in A\).
Bounded \(\exists m,M\in\mathbb{R}\;\forall a\in A,\;m\leq a\leq M\) Choose fixed bounds \(m,M\) independent of \(a\), then verify both inequalities for every \(a\in A\).
Unbounded above \(\forall M \in \mathbb{R}\; \exists a \in A,\; a > M\) Given \(M\), choose \(a\) in terms of \(M\).
Unbounded below \(\forall m \in \mathbb{R}\; \exists a \in A,\; a \lt m\) Given \(m\), choose \(a\) in terms of \(m\).
Proving a supremum \((\forall a \in A,\; a \leq s) \land (\forall \varepsilon > 0\; \exists a \in A,\; s-\varepsilon \lt a)\) Fix \(s\). Check the bound for arbitrary \(a\); then, given \(\varepsilon\), choose \(a\) in terms of \(\varepsilon\) and \(s\).
Proving an infimum \((\forall a \in A,\; t \leq a) \land (\forall \varepsilon > 0\; \exists a \in A,\; a \lt t+\varepsilon)\) Fix \(t\). Check the bound for arbitrary \(a\); then, given \(\varepsilon\), choose \(a\) in terms of \(\varepsilon\) and \(t\).
Disproving a supremum \((\exists \varepsilon > 0,\; s+\varepsilon \in A) \lor (\exists \varepsilon > 0\; \forall a \in A,\; a \leq s-\varepsilon)\) Fix \(s\) and choose a suitable \(\varepsilon\) in terms of \(s\). In the second alternative, the same \(\varepsilon\) must work for every \(a\).
Disproving an infimum \((\exists \varepsilon > 0,\; t-\varepsilon \in A) \lor (\exists \varepsilon > 0\; \forall a \in A,\; t+\varepsilon \leq a)\) Fix \(t\) and choose a suitable \(\varepsilon\) in terms of \(t\). In the second alternative, the same \(\varepsilon\) must work for every \(a\).
Using a known supremum \(s=\sup A \implies \forall \varepsilon > 0\; \exists x \in A,\; s-\varepsilon \lt x \leq s\) Choose a positive tolerance \(\varepsilon\); the corollary supplies \(x\) depending on it.
Using a known infimum \(t=\inf A \implies \forall \varepsilon > 0\; \exists x \in A,\; t \leq x \lt t+\varepsilon\) Choose a positive tolerance \(\varepsilon\); the corollary supplies \(x\) depending on it.

3. Finding Suitable Choices

We now systematically collect some useful techniques for solving the inequalities that arise in these proofs.

3.1. Bounding Expressions

Our goal is to find a choice that satisfies the required inequality, rather than to determine every possible solution. We can therefore replace the original inequality with a stronger, simpler condition: any solution of that condition will also solve the original problem. This is why estimates are useful even when they are not sharp.

To prove \(M > x\), it is enough to find an intermediate quantity \(D\) such that

\[ M \geq D > x. \]

Here we replace \(M\) by a smaller or equal quantity \(D\) and prove that even \(D\) is greater than \(x\). Thus, to show that an expression is sufficiently large, we can work with a lower bound for it.

Similarly, to prove \(m \lt x\), it is enough to find an intermediate quantity \(d\) such that

\[ m \leq d \lt x. \]

Here we replace \(m\) by a larger or equal quantity \(d\) and prove that even \(d\) is less than \(x\). Thus, to show that an expression is sufficiently small, we can work with an upper bound for it.

A useful way to remember the direction is: to prove it is large, make it smaller first; to prove it is small, make it larger first. The estimate makes the task stronger, so completing it guarantees the original inequality. Keep the strict inequality at the final comparison with \(x\); the intermediate estimate itself may be non-strict.

Let us return to the cube-root example and work backwards from the desired inequality. Suppose that \(s^3 > 2\). We want to choose \(\varepsilon > 0\) so that

\[ (s-\varepsilon)^3 > 2. \]

Expanding and rearranging gives

\[\begin{aligned} (s-\varepsilon)^3 > 2 &\iff s^3-3s^2\varepsilon+3s\varepsilon^2-\varepsilon^3 > 2 \\ &\iff 3s^2\varepsilon-3s\varepsilon^2+\varepsilon^3 \lt s^3-2. \end{aligned}\]

The right-hand side is a fixed positive gap. Our task is to make the expression on the left smaller than that gap. Following the principle above, we should bound the left-hand side from above: find a simpler expression \(U(\varepsilon)\) such that

\[ 3s^2\varepsilon-3s\varepsilon^2+\varepsilon^3 \leq U(\varepsilon) \lt s^3-2. \]

If even this larger expression lies below the gap, the original expression does too, and hence \((s-\varepsilon)^3 > 2\). This identifies the direction of the estimate. In the next subsection, we will discuss how to construct such an upper bound and use it to choose \(\varepsilon\).

3.2. Auxiliary Constraints

Returning to the inequality

\[ 3s^2\varepsilon-3s\varepsilon^2+\varepsilon^3 \lt s^3-2, \]

the main difficulty is the presence of higher powers of \(\varepsilon\). We would like to replace them with expressions that are linear in \(\varepsilon\), so that choosing \(\varepsilon\) becomes straightforward.

A standard idea is to impose the additional constraint

\[ 0 \lt \varepsilon \lt s. \]

Why are we allowed to do this? Here \(s > 0\) is fixed, and we are free to choose \(\varepsilon\): we only need one positive value that works. Restricting our search to \((0,s)\) is therefore legitimate, provided that we eventually find a value in this interval satisfying the original inequality. We are imposing a sufficient condition, not claiming that every possible solution must satisfy this restriction.

Under this constraint, we can replace selected factors of \(\varepsilon\) by the larger quantity \(s\). For example,

\[ \varepsilon^2 \leq s\varepsilon, \qquad \varepsilon^3 \leq s^2\varepsilon. \]

The direction of the estimate still matters. Multiplying an inequality by a negative coefficient reverses its direction, so replacing \(\varepsilon^2\) by \(s\varepsilon\) in the term \(-3s\varepsilon^2\) would make that term smaller. That would not give the upper bound we need.

For our present expression, it is more useful to group the last two terms. Since \(\varepsilon \lt s \lt 3s\),

\[\begin{aligned} 3s^2\varepsilon-3s\varepsilon^2+\varepsilon^3 &= 3s^2\varepsilon+\varepsilon^2(\varepsilon-3s) \\ &\leq 3s^2\varepsilon. \end{aligned}\]

Thus it is enough to require \(3s^2\varepsilon \lt s^3-2\), together with \(0\lt \varepsilon\lt s\). The choice used earlier,

\[ \varepsilon_1 = \min\left\{\frac{s}{2},\frac{s^3-2}{6s^2}\right\}, \]

satisfies both requirements: it is positive, is at most \(s/2\), and gives \(3s^2\varepsilon_1 \leq (s^3-2)/2 \lt s^3-2\).

The same constraint also explains the estimate in the other case, \(s^3\lt 2\). There all the error terms are positive, so replacing some factors of \(\varepsilon\) by \(s\) gives

\[\begin{aligned} 3s^2\varepsilon+3s\varepsilon^2+\varepsilon^3 &\leq 3s^2\varepsilon+3s^2\varepsilon+s^2\varepsilon \\ &= 7s^2\varepsilon. \end{aligned}\]

It is therefore enough to choose \(0\lt \varepsilon\lt s\) with \(7s^2\varepsilon\lt 2-s^3\). This leads to the earlier choice

\[ \varepsilon_2 = \min\left\{\frac{s}{2},\frac{2-s^3}{14s^2}\right\}. \]

Why not replace every occurrence of \(\varepsilon\) by \(s\)? Even when all coefficients are positive and the direction is valid, this can make the estimate too coarse. For instance, the last error expression would be bounded by \(7s^3\), a fixed quantity that does not become smaller as we decrease \(\varepsilon\). The resulting sufficient condition \(7s^3\lt 2-s^3\) need not hold; indeed, it fails when \(s\geq1\), as in our example. This would leave us with an unusable estimate, even though suitable small values of \(\varepsilon\) exist.

The useful principle is to keep one factor of \(\varepsilon\) and bound the remaining factors. This turns higher-order terms into a constant multiple of \(\varepsilon\), preserving our ability to make the error as small as needed.

The following table collects some common auxiliary constraints. Throughout, we assume \(\varepsilon > 0\).

Goal Constraint Consequence
Reduce higher powers \(\varepsilon \lt 1\) \(\varepsilon^3 \lt \varepsilon^2 \lt \varepsilon\)
Reduce higher powers using a fixed scale \(\varepsilon \lt r\), where \(r>0\) \(\varepsilon^2 \lt r\varepsilon,\quad \varepsilon^3 \lt r^2\varepsilon\)
Preserve positivity and compare with the original value \(\varepsilon \lt x/2\), where \(x>0\) \(\displaystyle \frac{x}{2} \lt x-\varepsilon \lt x+\varepsilon \lt \frac{3x}{2}\)
Control a denominator or reciprocal \(\varepsilon \lt x/2\), where \(x>0\) \(\displaystyle \frac{1}{x-\varepsilon} \lt \frac{2}{x}\)
Stay inside an interval \(\varepsilon \lt \min\{x-a,b-x\}\), where \(a \lt x \lt b\) \(a \lt x-\varepsilon \lt x+\varepsilon \lt b\)
Preserve the order of two values \(\varepsilon \lt (y-x)/2\), where \(x \lt y\) \(x+\varepsilon \lt y-\varepsilon\)
Make an error smaller than an existing gap \(\varepsilon \lt d/C\), where \(C,d>0\) \(C\varepsilon \lt d\)
Keep a factor away from zero \(\varepsilon \lt 1/(2C)\), where \(C>0\) \(\displaystyle 1-C\varepsilon > \frac{1}{2},\quad \frac{1}{1-C\varepsilon} \lt 2\)

3.3. Choosing a Solution

Once we have listed all the constraints, how do we choose a value satisfying them simultaneously? For finitely many real bounds \(a_1,\ldots,a_n\) and \(b_1,\ldots,b_n\), where \(n\) is a positive integer, maxima and minima provide explicit choices.

Requirement Conditions A suitable choice
\(M > a_i\quad (1\leq i\leq n)\) Finitely many real lower bounds \(\displaystyle M=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}a_i+1\)
\(m \lt b_i\quad (1\leq i\leq n)\) Finitely many real upper bounds \(\displaystyle m=\min_{1\leq i\leq n}b_i-1\)
\(a_i \lt \varepsilon \lt b_i\quad (1\leq i\leq n)\) Finitely many real bounds, with \(\displaystyle \max_{1\leq i\leq n}a_i \lt \min_{1\leq i\leq n}b_i\) \(\displaystyle \varepsilon=\frac{1}{2}\left(\max_{1\leq i\leq n}a_i+\min_{1\leq i\leq n}b_i\right)\)

In the last row, we take the midpoint between the largest lower bound and the smallest upper bound. If we also require \(\varepsilon>0\), include \(0\) among the lower bounds before applying the rule.

Some constraints specify the set to which a chosen value must belong. Let \(x\in\mathbb{R}\) and continue to require \(\varepsilon>0\). To arrange \(x+\varepsilon\in S\), choose a point \(y\in S\) above \(x\) and set \(\varepsilon=y-x\). To arrange \(x-\varepsilon\in S\), choose a point \(y\in S\) below \(x\) and set \(\varepsilon=x-y\). The two signs describe separate choices; they need not use the same \(\varepsilon\).

For integer constraints, the integer-bracketing lemma gives an integer \(k\) with \(k-1\leq x\lt k\). Thus \(k\) lies strictly above \(x\), while \(k-2\) lies strictly below it. For positive integers above \(x\), we can instead apply the Archimedean property directly.

Target set \(S\) Choosing \(x+\varepsilon\in S\) Choosing \(x-\varepsilon\in S\)
\(\mathbb{Z}\) Choose \(k\in\mathbb{Z}\) with \(k-1\leq x\lt k\); set \(\varepsilon=k-x\). With the same \(k\), set \(\varepsilon=x-(k-2)\).
\(\mathbb{Z}_{+}=\{1,2,\ldots\}\) By the Archimedean property, choose a positive integer \(k>x\); set \(\varepsilon=k-x\). Possible exactly when \(x>1\): choose \(\varepsilon=x-1\), giving \(x-\varepsilon=1\).
\(n\mathbb{Z}\), where \(n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\) Apply the lemma to \(x/n\): choose \(k-1\leq x/n\lt k\) and set \(\varepsilon=nk-x\). With the same \(k\), set \(\varepsilon=x-n(k-2)\).
\(\mathbb{Q}\) Choose a rational \(q>x\) and set \(\varepsilon=q-x\). Choose a rational \(q\lt x\) and set \(\varepsilon=x-q\).
\(\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}\) Choose an irrational \(u>x\) and set \(\varepsilon=u-x\). Choose an irrational \(u\lt x\) and set \(\varepsilon=x-u\).

If \(\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,\ldots\}\), its constraints are the same as those for \(\mathbb{Z}_{+}\). If the convention is \(\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}\), the subtraction case is possible exactly when \(x>0\): take \(\varepsilon=x\), so that \(x-\varepsilon=0\).

For the last two rows, both the rationals and the irrationals are dense in \(\mathbb{R}\): every nonempty open interval contains a point of each set. Consequently, if we also need \(0\lt\varepsilon\lt\delta\) for a given \(\delta>0\), we can choose the target point in \((x,x+\delta)\) or \((x-\delta,x)\), respectively. One way to obtain an irrational point in an interval \((a,b)\) is to choose a rational \(q\in(a-\sqrt{2},b-\sqrt{2})\); then \(q+\sqrt{2}\) is irrational and belongs to \((a,b)\).

Integer constraints behave differently: a prescribed short interval may contain no integer or no multiple of \(n\). When combining these membership requirements with bounds on \(\varepsilon\), we must check that the corresponding interval actually contains an allowed target point.
