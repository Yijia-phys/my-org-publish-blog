1. Foundations

Let us begin with a brief review of the definitions.

Definition 1 — Boundedness

Let A⊆RA \subseteq \mathbb{R}A⊆R. We say that AAA is bounded above if there exists M∈RM \in \mathbb{R}M∈R such that a≤Ma \leq Ma≤M for every a∈Aa \in Aa∈A. Any such MMM is called an upper bound of AAA.

Similarly, AAA is bounded below if there exists m∈Rm \in \mathbb{R}m∈R such that m≤am \leq am≤a for every a∈Aa \in Aa∈A. Any such mmm is called a lower bound of AAA.

The set AAA is bounded if it is both bounded above and bounded below. Thus,

A is bounded  ⟺  (A is bounded above)∧(A is bounded below). A \text{ is bounded} \iff \bigl(A \text{ is bounded above}\bigr) \land \bigl(A \text{ is bounded below}\bigr). A is bounded⟺(A is bounded above)∧(A is bounded below).

In particular, the empty set is bounded: every real number is both an upper bound and a lower bound of ∅\varnothing∅, since there are no elements that could violate either condition.

Definition 2 — Supremum and infimum

Let A⊆RA \subseteq \mathbb{R}A⊆R. A real number sss is the supremum (or least upper bound) of AAA, written s=sup⁡As = \sup As=supA, if:

  1. a≤sa \leq sa≤s for every a∈Aa \in Aa∈A;
  2. s≤Ms \leq Ms≤M for every upper bound M∈RM \in \mathbb{R}M∈R of AAA.

Similarly, a real number ttt is the infimum (or greatest lower bound) of AAA, written t=inf⁡At = \inf At=infA, if:

  1. t≤at \leq at≤a for every a∈Aa \in Aa∈A;
  2. m≤tm \leq tm≤t for every lower bound m∈Rm \in \mathbb{R}m∈R of AAA.

To include unbounded sets and the empty set, we also allow values in the extended real line R‾=R∪{−∞,+∞}\overline{\mathbb{R}} = \mathbb{R} \cup \{-\infty,+\infty\}R=R∪{−∞,+∞}, ordered by −∞<x<+∞-\infty \lt x \lt +\infty−∞<x<+∞ for every x∈Rx \in \mathbb{R}x∈R. We adopt the conventions

sup⁡A=+∞if A is not bounded above,inf⁡A=−∞if A is not bounded below,sup⁡∅=−∞,inf⁡∅=+∞.\begin{aligned} \sup A &= +\infty &&\text{if } A \text{ is not bounded above}, \\ \inf A &= -\infty &&\text{if } A \text{ is not bounded below}, \\ \sup \varnothing &= -\infty, \qquad &\inf \varnothing &= +\infty. \end{aligned}supAinfAsup∅​=+∞=−∞=−∞,​inf∅​if A is not bounded above,if A is not bounded below,=+∞.​

These conventions agree with taking the least upper bound and greatest lower bound in R‾\overline{\mathbb{R}}R. The empty set has no supremum or infimum in R\mathbb{R}R itself.

Axiom 3 — Completeness of the real numbers

Every nonempty subset of R\mathbb{R}R that is bounded above has a supremum in R\mathbb{R}R.

Every nonempty subset of R\mathbb{R}R that is bounded below has an infimum in R\mathbb{R}R.

The second statement follows from the first by applying it to −A={−a:a∈A}-A = \{-a : a \in A\}−A={−a:a∈A} and using inf⁡A=−sup⁡(−A)\inf A = -\sup(-A)infA=−sup(−A). We state both together here for ease of reference.

Theorem 4 — Archimedean property

For every x∈Rx \in \mathbb{R}x∈R, there exists a positive integer nnn such that x<nx \lt nx<n. In other words, the positive integers are not bounded above in R\mathbb{R}R.

Lemma 5 — Integer bracketing

For every x∈Rx \in \mathbb{R}x∈R, there exists an integer n∈Zn \in \mathbb{Z}n∈Z such that

n−1≤x<n. n - 1 \leq x \lt n. n−1≤x<n.

We will not go into further proofs or explanations here; for details, see the notes.

2. Proof Strategies

We now examine in detail how to prove the various properties introduced above.

2.1. Boundedness

To prove boundedness, we find fixed real bounds that work for every element of the set.

Remark 6

Let A⊆RA\subseteq\mathbb{R}A⊆R. Then

A is bounded above  ⟺  ∃M∈R  ∀a∈A,a≤M,A is bounded below  ⟺  ∃m∈R  ∀a∈A,m≤a,A is bounded  ⟺  ∃m,M∈R  ∀a∈A,m≤a≤M.\begin{aligned} A\text{ is bounded above} &\iff \exists M\in\mathbb{R}\;\forall a\in A,\quad a\leq M, \\ A\text{ is bounded below} &\iff \exists m\in\mathbb{R}\;\forall a\in A,\quad m\leq a, \\ A\text{ is bounded} &\iff \exists m,M\in\mathbb{R}\;\forall a\in A,\quad m\leq a\leq M. \end{aligned}A is bounded aboveA is bounded belowA is bounded​⟺∃M∈R∀a∈A,a≤M,⟺∃m∈R∀a∈A,m≤a,⟺∃m,M∈R∀a∈A,m≤a≤M.​

The bounds may depend on the set and its fixed parameters, but must not depend on the individual element aaa. We choose the bounds first and then verify the inequalities for arbitrary a∈Aa\in Aa∈A; there is no need to find the best possible bounds.

2.2. Unboundedness

First, negating the definitions of boundedness above and below gives the following characterizations.

Remark 7

Let A⊆RA \subseteq \mathbb{R}A⊆R. Then

A is unbounded above  ⟺  ¬(∃M∈R  ∀a∈A,  a≤M)  ⟺  ∀M∈R  ∃a∈A,  a>M,A is unbounded below  ⟺  ¬(∃m∈R  ∀a∈A,  m≤a)  ⟺  ∀m∈R  ∃a∈A,  a<m.\begin{aligned} A \text{ is unbounded above} &\iff \neg\bigl(\exists M \in \mathbb{R}\; \forall a \in A,\; a \leq M\bigr) \\ &\iff \forall M \in \mathbb{R}\; \exists a \in A,\; a > M, \\ A \text{ is unbounded below} &\iff \neg\bigl(\exists m \in \mathbb{R}\; \forall a \in A,\; m \leq a\bigr) \\ &\iff \forall m \in \mathbb{R}\; \exists a \in A,\; a \lt m. \end{aligned}A is unbounded aboveA is unbounded below​⟺¬(∃M∈R∀a∈A,a≤M)⟺∀M∈R∃a∈A,a>M,⟺¬(∃m∈R∀a∈A,m≤a)⟺∀m∈R∃a∈A,a<m.​

In other words, to prove that AAA is unbounded above, we start with an arbitrary proposed upper bound M∈RM \in \mathbb{R}M∈R and find an element a∈Aa \in Aa∈A with a>Ma > Ma>M. Our choice of aaa may depend on MMM: when giving an explicit construction, we express aaa in terms of MMM and verify both that a∈Aa \in Aa∈A and that a>Ma > Ma>M.

Likewise, to prove that AAA is unbounded below, we start with an arbitrary m∈Rm \in \mathbb{R}m∈R and choose a∈Aa \in Aa∈A, depending on mmm, such that a<ma \lt ma<m. The order of the quantifiers matters: the proposed bound is given first, and we then choose an element that exceeds it or falls below it.

Let us illustrate this with a simple example.

Example 8 — The real line is unbounded

The set A={x∣x∈R}=RA = \{x \mid x \in \mathbb{R}\} = \mathbb{R}A={x∣x∈R}=R is unbounded both above and below.

Proof.

Given any M∈RM \in \mathbb{R}M∈R, choose a=M+1a = M + 1a=M+1. Then a∈Aa \in Aa∈A and a>Ma > Ma>M, so AAA is unbounded above.

Similarly, given any m∈Rm \in \mathbb{R}m∈R, choose a=m−1a = m - 1a=m−1. Then a∈Aa \in Aa∈A and a<ma \lt ma<m, so AAA is unbounded below. Hence AAA is unbounded.

□

2.3. Identifying a Supremum or Infimum

To prove that a real number sss is the supremum of AAA, we must show that it is an upper bound and that it is the least upper bound. For the second part, we show that every number strictly smaller than sss, written as s−εs - \varepsilons−ε with ε>0\varepsilon > 0ε>0, fails to be an upper bound.

Remark 9

Let A⊆RA \subseteq \mathbb{R}A⊆R and s,t∈Rs,t \in \mathbb{R}s,t∈R. Then s=sup⁡As = \sup As=supA if and only if both of the following hold:

  1. sss is an upper bound: ∀a∈A,a≤s. \forall a \in A,\quad a \leq s. ∀a∈A,a≤s.
  2. For every ε>0\varepsilon > 0ε>0, s−εs - \varepsilons−ε is not an upper bound: ∀ε>0  ∃a∈A,s−ε<a. \forall \varepsilon > 0\; \exists a \in A,\quad s - \varepsilon \lt a. ∀ε>0∃a∈A,s−ε<a.

Similarly, t=inf⁡At = \inf At=infA if and only if both of the following hold:

  1. ttt is a lower bound: ∀a∈A,t≤a. \forall a \in A,\quad t \leq a. ∀a∈A,t≤a.
  2. For every ε>0\varepsilon > 0ε>0, t+εt + \varepsilont+ε is not a lower bound: ∀ε>0  ∃a∈A,a<t+ε. \forall \varepsilon > 0\; \exists a \in A,\quad a \lt t + \varepsilon. ∀ε>0∃a∈A,a<t+ε.

The two parts require different approaches. To establish the bound, take an arbitrary a∈Aa \in Aa∈A and prove the required inequality. To establish that the bound is the least or greatest one, take an arbitrary ε>0\varepsilon > 0ε>0 and find a suitable a∈Aa \in Aa∈A. Here the candidate sss or ttt is fixed, and our choice of aaa may depend on ε\varepsilonε: in an explicit construction, we express aaa in terms of ε\varepsilonε (and the fixed candidate sss or ttt).

For a supremum, the chosen element must satisfy s−ε<a≤ss - \varepsilon \lt a \leq ss−ε<a≤s; for an infimum, it must satisfy t≤a<t+εt \leq a \lt t + \varepsilont≤a<t+ε. In each case, we must verify that the chosen element belongs to AAA. The element need not equal the supremum or infimum; it only needs to lie within the prescribed distance on the appropriate side.

Let

First, for every x>0x > 0x>0, we have 1/x>01/x > 01/x>0, so 1−1/x<11 - 1/x \lt 11−1/x<1. Thus every a∈Aa \in Aa∈A satisfies a≤1a \leq 1a≤1, and 111 is an upper bound of AAA.

There is also a simpler way to identify a supremum when the proposed upper bound belongs to the set itself.

Theorem 11 — Attained bounds are suprema and infima

Let A⊆RA \subseteq \mathbb{R}A⊆R and s,t∈Rs,t \in \mathbb{R}s,t∈R. If sss is an upper bound of AAA and s∈As \in As∈A, then sup⁡A=s\sup A = ssupA=s.

Similarly, if ttt is a lower bound of AAA and t∈At \in At∈A, then inf⁡A=t\inf A = tinfA=t.

The converse does not hold: s=sup⁡As = \sup As=supA does not imply that s∈As \in As∈A. In the preceding example, sup⁡A=1\sup A = 1supA=1, but 1∉A1 \notin A1∈/A, since 1−1/x<11 - 1/x \lt 11−1/x<1 for every x>0x > 0x>0.

2.4. Ruling Out a Candidate Supremum or Infimum

Taking the contrapositives of the necessary conditions in Section 2.3 gives two ways to rule out a proposed supremum or infimum. It is enough for either condition to fail.

Remark 12

Let A⊆RA \subseteq \mathbb{R}A⊆R and let s,t∈Rs,t \in \mathbb{R}s,t∈R be arbitrary. Then sss is not the supremum of AAA if either of the following holds:

  1. There exists ε>0\varepsilon > 0ε>0 such that s+ε∈As + \varepsilon \in As+ε∈A, so sss is not an upper bound.
  2. There exists ε>0\varepsilon > 0ε>0 such that s−εs - \varepsilons−ε is an upper bound of AAA, so sss cannot be the least upper bound.

In logical notation,

[(∃ε>0,  s+ε∈A)∨(∃ε>0  ∀a∈A,  a≤s−ε)]  ⟺  s≠sup⁡A.\begin{aligned} \Bigl[\bigl(\exists \varepsilon > 0,\; s + \varepsilon \in A\bigr) \lor \bigl(\exists \varepsilon > 0\; \forall a \in A,\; a \leq s - \varepsilon\bigr)\Bigr] \\ \iff s \neq \sup A. \end{aligned}[(∃ε>0,s+ε∈A)∨(∃ε>0∀a∈A,a≤s−ε)]⟺s=supA.​

Similarly, ttt is not the infimum of AAA if either of the following holds:

  1. There exists ε>0\varepsilon > 0ε>0 such that t−ε∈At - \varepsilon \in At−ε∈A, so ttt is not a lower bound.
  2. There exists ε>0\varepsilon > 0ε>0 such that t+εt + \varepsilont+ε is a lower bound of AAA, so ttt cannot be the greatest lower bound.

In logical notation,

[(∃ε>0,  t−ε∈A)∨(∃ε>0  ∀a∈A,  t+ε≤a)]  ⟺  t≠inf⁡A.\begin{aligned} \Bigl[\bigl(\exists \varepsilon > 0,\; t - \varepsilon \in A\bigr) \lor \bigl(\exists \varepsilon > 0\; \forall a \in A,\; t + \varepsilon \leq a\bigr)\Bigr] \\ \iff t \neq \inf A. \end{aligned}[(∃ε>0,t−ε∈A)∨(∃ε>0∀a∈A,t+ε≤a)]⟺t=infA.​

The dependence has now changed. In Section 2.3, we were given an arbitrary ε>0\varepsilon > 0ε>0 and had to choose an element aaa in terms of it. Here the candidate sss or ttt is fixed, and we only need to find one suitable ε>0\varepsilon > 0ε>0, chosen in terms of that candidate, to rule it out. In the second case, this single choice must work for every a∈Aa \in Aa∈A, since we must show that the shifted candidate is still a bound.

2.5. Using Known Suprema and Infima

Corollary 14 — Approximating suprema and infima by elements of the set

Let A⊆RA \subseteq \mathbb{R}A⊆R and s,t∈Rs,t \in \mathbb{R}s,t∈R.

If s=sup⁡As = \sup As=supA, then for every ε>0\varepsilon > 0ε>0 there exists x∈Ax \in Ax∈A such that

s−ε<x≤s. s - \varepsilon \lt x \leq s. s−ε<x≤s.

Similarly, if t=inf⁡At = \inf At=infA, then for every ε>0\varepsilon > 0ε>0 there exists x∈Ax \in Ax∈A such that

t≤x<t+ε. t \leq x \lt t + \varepsilon. t≤x<t+ε.

In logical notation,

s=sup⁡A  ⟹  ∀ε>0  ∃x∈A,s−ε<x≤s,t=inf⁡A  ⟹  ∀ε>0  ∃x∈A,t≤x<t+ε.\begin{aligned} s = \sup A &\implies \forall \varepsilon > 0\; \exists x \in A,\quad s - \varepsilon \lt x \leq s, \\ t = \inf A &\implies \forall \varepsilon > 0\; \exists x \in A,\quad t \leq x \lt t + \varepsilon. \end{aligned}s=supAt=infA​⟹∀ε>0∃x∈A,s−ε<x≤s,⟹∀ε>0∃x∈A,t≤x<t+ε.​

Once we know that s=sup⁡As = \sup As=supA or t=inf⁡At = \inf At=infA, we can use the corresponding approximation property as an available hypothesis in another proof. We choose a positive tolerance suited to the problem, and the corollary supplies an element of AAA within that tolerance of the known supremum or infimum.

3. Finding Suitable Choices

We now systematically collect some useful techniques for solving the inequalities that arise in these proofs.

3.1. Bounding Expressions

Our goal is to find a choice that satisfies the required inequality, rather than to determine every possible solution. We can therefore replace the original inequality with a stronger, simpler condition: any solution of that condition will also solve the original problem. This is why estimates are useful even when they are not sharp.

To prove M>xM > xM>x, it is enough to find an intermediate quantity DDD such that

M≥D>x. M \geq D > x. M≥D>x.

Here we replace MMM by a smaller or equal quantity DDD and prove that even DDD is greater than xxx. Thus, to show that an expression is sufficiently large, we can work with a lower bound for it.

Similarly, to prove m<xm \lt xm<x, it is enough to find an intermediate quantity ddd such that

m≤d<x. m \leq d \lt x. m≤d<x.

Here we replace mmm by a larger or equal quantity ddd and prove that even ddd is less than xxx. Thus, to show that an expression is sufficiently small, we can work with an upper bound for it.

A useful way to remember the direction is: to prove it is large, make it smaller first; to prove it is small, make it larger first. The estimate makes the task stronger, so completing it guarantees the original inequality. Keep the strict inequality at the final comparison with xxx; the intermediate estimate itself may be non-strict.

Let us return to the cube-root example and work backwards from the desired inequality. Suppose that s3>2s^3 > 2s3>2. We want to choose ε>0\varepsilon > 0ε>0 so that
